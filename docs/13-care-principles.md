# CARE principles for Indigenous data governance

*Put people and purpose at the centre of your data.*

Does your collection include Indigenous data, such as cultural materials, knowledge, languages, images, recordings or information about Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, Pacific Islander peoples or other Indigenous peoples? If so, you should consider applying the CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance.

The CARE principles were developed by the [Global Indigenous Data Alliance (GIDA)](https://www.gida-global.org/careprinciples). They promote ethical and culturally respectful data practices that put Indigenous peoples' rights, interests and self-determination first.

GIDA created the CARE principles in response to the growing uptake of the [FAIR principles](07-making-your-data-fair.md) by data infrastructure builders around the world. FAIR focuses on making data easy to share and reuse, but it does not address the rights and interests of Indigenous peoples. CARE fills that gap.

## The four CARE principles

CARE stands for **C**ollective benefit, **A**uthority to control, **R**esponsibility and **E**thics.

### Collective benefit

Data ecosystems should be designed and run so that Indigenous peoples can benefit from the data. In practice this means:

- Considering how your collection can deliver value back to the communities it relates to
- Supporting Indigenous innovation, governance and decision-making through data
- Making sure benefits are shared fairly, not only with researchers or institutions

### Authority to control

Indigenous peoples' rights and interests in Indigenous data must be recognised, and their authority to control that data must be supported. This includes:

- Recognising that communities have the right to decide how data about them, their lands and their cultures is collected, described, accessed and used
- Working with communities on decisions about what is published, what is restricted and who can see it
- Supporting Indigenous governance of data, rather than treating consultation as a one-off step

### Responsibility

People who work with Indigenous data are responsible for showing how that data is used to support Indigenous self-determination and collective benefit. This includes:

- Being open and accountable about how the data is used, and by whom
- Building positive, ongoing relationships with the communities connected to the data
- Building capability, both within communities and within your own team

### Ethics

Indigenous peoples' rights and wellbeing should be the main concern at every stage of the data life cycle and across the whole data ecosystem. This includes:

- Minimising harm and maximising benefit throughout collection, description, publication and reuse
- Ensuring ethical decisions reflect Indigenous values, not only institutional requirements
- Planning for future use, including what happens to the data if the collection changes or is retired

CARE does not replace FAIR. The two sets of principles work together:

| FAIR | CARE |
|------|------|
| Data-centred | People- and purpose-centred |
| Makes data easier to find, share and reuse | Ensures data is used ethically and for the benefit of Indigenous peoples |
| Asks *"Can this data be used?"* | Asks *"Should this data be used, by whom, and for what?"* |

The CARE principles recognise the important role of data in advancing Indigenous innovation and self-determination. They help make sure that movements such as open data respect the people and purposes behind the data. Remember that FAIR does not mean open: with Indigenous data, applying CARE may mean some material is restricted or not published at all.

## A global standard

The CARE principles were drafted on 8 November 2018 in Gaborone, Botswana, at a workshop on Indigenous data sovereignty principles held alongside International Data Week and the Research Data Alliance Plenary. They apply to research involving Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander peoples, and can also guide research related to South Pacific Islander peoples and other Indigenous peoples around the world.

## Applying CARE in Curated Collections

The following suggestions show how CARE can shape the way you build and publish a collection in Omeka-S. They are a starting point, not a substitute for working directly with the relevant communities.

| Principle | Things to consider in Omeka-S |
|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Collective benefit | Can community members easily find and use the collection? Is the site's language, structure and navigation meaningful to them? |
| Authority to control | Have communities been involved in deciding which items are public? Use item and media **visibility settings** (public/private) and **private fields** in CSV Import to keep restricted material off the public site. |
| Responsibility | Record provenance clearly, including the communities, knowledge holders and contributors connected to each item. Document who to contact about the collection. |
| Ethics | Add cultural warnings where needed (for example, for images, voices or names of people who have died). Include clear rights and access statements in your metadata, such as `dcterms:rights` and `dcterms:accessRights`. |

You may also want to look at [Local Contexts](https://localcontexts.org/), which provides Traditional Knowledge (TK) and Biocultural (BC) Labels and Notices that communities can apply to their materials to express cultural rights and protocols.

> 💡 **Tip:** Check the [Pre-publication checklist](08-pre-publication-checklist.md) before making any collection containing Indigenous data public.
